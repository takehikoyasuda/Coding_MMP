Worked examples

Seven worked examples that these packages compute, each with the Macaulay2 session that computes it.

Every example has two parts. First a short explanation of the mathematics. Then the session that does the computation. The sessions are real: the input files in examples/*.m2 are given to Macaulay2, and the output is saved exactly as Macaulay2 printed it. Each session is folded, with its main results shown on the fold. Click one to read all of it. The algorithms and their proofs are in the paper (arXiv:2603.13703).

In Examples 1 to 6, $X$ is a normal projective threefold and $R$ is its homogeneous coordinate ring. Example 7 relaxes that: some of the variables are given degree zero, their coordinates land in $R_0$, and $X = \Proj R$ is then projective over the affine $\Spec R_0$ rather than over a point. In all of them $K_X$ is the canonical divisor and $H$ is an ample Cartier divisor, ample over the base where there is one. The nef threshold is

$$\lambda = \min\{\,t \ge 0 \;:\; K_X + tH \text{ is nef}\,\}.$$

$X$ has to be normal and log terminal. The package assumes this. It does not check it.

In the paper, $X = \Proj R$ is monograded with $R_0 = k$. A bigraded ring appears only as the graph of a morphism, which is how the Stein factorization is computed. Examples 1 to 6 stay inside that setting. Example 7 keeps the single grading but drops $R_0 = k$, which is the relative setting; the package also has entry points that take a multigraded variety directly, and those go beyond the paper too, but this page does not use them.

1. Projective space

Here $X = \PP^3$ and $K_X = -4H$, where $H$ is the hyperplane class. The example asks three questions.

Is $K_X$ nef? No. The package tests the divisor $K_X + \tfrac12 H$. It checks the first six multiples of that divisor, and none of them is base point free. Six is enough: the effective base point free theorem says so for this case. So $K_X + \tfrac12 H$ is not nef. If $K_X$ were nef, then $K_X + \tfrac12 H$ would be nef too, because $H$ is ample. So $K_X$ is not nef.

What is the nef threshold? $K_X + tH = (t-4)H$, which is nef exactly when $t \ge 4$. So $\lambda = 4$, and the package finds $4$.

What does $K_X + \lambda H$ contract? $K_X + 4H = 0$. It is base point free, and its linear system sends $X$ to one point. This is a fibre type contraction, and the dimension drops by $3$. The MMP driver stops here, after one step, at a $K$-negative fibration.

2. A quintic threefold

$X$ is a smooth quintic in $\PP^4$. By adjunction $K_X = (5-5)H = 0$.

The package tests the pluricanonical divisors first. The first one is $K_X$ itself, and it is base point free. So $K_X$ is nef and $X$ is already a minimal model. The driver stops after zero steps.

3. The Segre threefold

$X = \PP^1 \times \PP^2$, embedded in $\PP^5$ by the Segre map. So $R$ is the quotient by the $2 \times 2$ minors of a $2 \times 3$ matrix of variables.

Here $K_X = \OO(-2,-3)$ and $H = \OO(1,1)$, so $K_X + tH = \OO(t-2,\,t-3)$. This is nef exactly when $t \ge 3$. The package computes $\lambda = 3$, and then computes the contraction given by

$$K_X + 3H = \OO(1,0).$$

The linear system of $\OO(1,0)$ is the projection $X \to \PP^1$. The package finds a fibre type contraction with target dimension $1$.

This time the target is not a point, so there is a Stein factorization to compute. The bound that certifies it is computed too, not assumed. Here the factorization is trivial, because $\mathrm{pr}_1$ already has connected fibres. Example 4 is the same $X$ with a map whose fibres are not connected.

4. A map with disconnected fibres

Stein factorization writes a map $f : Y \to W$ as $f = g \circ h$, where $h$ has connected fibres and $g$ is finite. In examples 3 and 5 the finite map $g$ turns out to be an isomorphism, so the factorization does nothing. This example is one where it does something.

Take $Y = \PP^1 \times \PP^2$ again, and the map

$$f : Y \to \PP^1, \qquad ([s_0:s_1],\,x) \longmapsto [s_0^2 : s_1^2].$$

Over a point of $\PP^1$ other than $[1:0]$ and $[0:1]$, the square map has two preimages, so the fibre of $f$ is two disjoint copies of $\PP^2$. Over those two points the fibre is one copy. The session checks this first, with no help from the package: it decomposes the fibre over $t_0 = t_1$ and gets two pieces, and the fibre over $t_1 = 0$ and gets one.

So $f$ does not have connected fibres, and the factorization has to be

$$Y \;\xrightarrow{\ h = \mathrm{pr}_1\ }\; \PP^1 \;\xrightarrow{\ g = \text{squaring}\ }\; \PP^1 .$$

The package finds exactly this. The middle variety $Z$ has dimension $1$, and $Y$ has dimension $3$, so $h$ has relative dimension $2$. The target also has dimension $1$, so $g$ is finite. And the Stein algebra has rank $2$ over the coordinate ring of the target, which is the degree of $g$. Degree $2$ means $g$ is not an isomorphism.

The same rank is $1$ in example 5, where $g$ is an isomorphism.

5. Blowing up a line, and a two-step program

$Y$ is the blow up of $\PP^3$ along a line, and $f : Y \to \PP^3$ is the map. The package is given one thing only: a parametrization of the graph of $f$. Nothing about $f$ is stated. The graph lives in a bigraded ring, where the first block of variables is the source and the second block is the target. This is how the paper writes a morphism.

Stein factorization. Write $f = g \circ h$, where $h$ has connected fibres and $g$ is finite. The package computes the coordinate ring of the middle variety $Z$. Two things come out of it. First, $\dim Z = 3$. Since $\dim Y = 3$ as well, the dimension does not drop, so $f$ is birational. Second, the ring of $Z$ is monograded, so the program can go on from $Z$ without any further work.

Is the contraction small? A birational contraction is called small when its exceptional locus has codimension $2$ or more. A small contraction needs a flip. A divisorial one does not. The package finds the exceptional locus as the support of $\bigwedge^2 \Omega_{Y/Z}$.

For this $f$ the exceptional locus is the exceptional divisor $E$. The package finds dimension $2$ and codimension $1$, so the contraction is divisorial.

The same test then runs on a small contraction, for comparison. Take the ordinary double point $\{g_0g_1 = g_2g_3\}$ and blow up the ideal $(g_0,g_2)$. This is the standard small resolution. Its exceptional locus is a curve, of dimension $1$ and codimension $2$, so that contraction is small. Example 6 computes a flip for a case like this one.

The program. The relative canonical model over $Z$ is the identity, so no flip is needed and the step is divisorial. The driver then continues from $Z$. It repeats example 1: $\lambda = 4$, and the contraction to a point. The whole program is

$$\mathrm{Bl}_L\PP^3 \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{divisorial}\ }\; \PP^3 \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{fibration}\ }\; \text{point},$$

two steps, ending at a $K$-negative fibration. Every ring in the chain is monograded, and the only bigraded ring is the graph of $f$.

6. A toric flip

Take four rays in $N = \ZZ^3$:

$$v_1 = e_1,\quad v_2 = e_2,\quad v_3 = e_3,\quad v_4 = (1,1,-2).$$

They satisfy one relation, $v_1 + v_2 = 2v_3 + v_4$. Four rays with a single relation of this shape form a circuit. The cone $\sigma$ they span can be triangulated in two ways, and the two results differ by a flip. The cone itself, with no triangulation, is the target of the flipping contraction.

$\sigma$ is not $\QQ$-Gorenstein. To see this, look for an $m$ with $\langle m, v_i \rangle = 1$ for all four rays. The first three rays force $m = (1,1,1)$, but $\langle (1,1,1), v_4 \rangle = 0$. So there is no such $m$.

The example builds this variety in Macaulay2. The dual cone has a Hilbert basis of five elements with three relations, and one more variable $w$ makes a projective threefold $W$. The flip is then computed as the relative canonical model

$$W^{+} = \Proj \bigoplus_{m \ge 0} H^0\bigl(W, \OO_W(mK_W)\bigr).$$

The session checks that this is a real flip: the result is not the identity and not an isomorphism, but it has the same dimension as $W$. The package also writes down the inverse rational map $W \dashrightarrow W^{+}$, checks that its equations hold, and finds its base locus $V(y_4,y_5)$. Recorded as an MMP step, this is a flipping step.

7. A flip the program finds by itself, over an affine base

Example 6 is handed the flipping contraction; this one is given only a ring. Giving an ambient variable degree zero puts its coordinate in $R_0$, so $X = \Proj R$ becomes projective over the affine $\Spec R_0$ — where the three-fold flips are, a flipping contraction contracting a curve to a point of an affine variety.

The first input is Example 6's circuit $v_1 + v_2 = 2v_3 + v_4$, read through its Cox ring: $\mathrm{Cl}(X) = \ZZ$ with $\deg(z_1,z_2,z_3,z_4) = (1,1,-2,-1)$, and the two triangulations are the two geometric invariant theory quotients, $Y$ from the monomials of nonpositive weight and $Z$ from the nonnegative ones. Each has one wall curve $C$, with $K_Y \cdot C = -1$ and $K_Z \cdot C = +1$, and $H \cdot C = 1$. $Y$ has one $\tfrac12(1,1,1)$ point, so its canonical index is $2$ and the threshold is $\tfrac12$. At that threshold $C$ — a curve, not a divisor — contracts to the singular point of $\Spec R_0$. A small contraction, so the step is a flip, and the model becomes $Z$, where $K$ is nef. A degree-preserving relabelling carries the ring the program returns onto $Z$.

$Y$ is presented by the second Veronese of its section ring, because the ring itself needs a generator of degree two, and under a weighted grading the Cartier test over-reports — a divisor can be locally free on the punctured cone without being invertible on $Y$, as $\OO(1)$ is on $\PP(1,1,2)$ — which returns the index as $1$ where it is $2$.

Three more inputs, each contracting something different. $\mathrm{Bl}_0(\AA^3)$, as $\Proj$ of the Rees algebra of $(a,b,c)$, has $K = 2E$ and $\OO(1) = -E$, so the threshold is $2$; there the exceptional surface $E$ contracts to the origin of $\AA^3$, and the step is divisorial. The weights $(1,1,-1,-3)$ give another flip, of canonical index three and threshold $\tfrac23$. And on $\mathrm{Bl}_0(\AA^2) \times \PP^1$ over $\AA^2$ with $\OO(1,3)$ the thresholds are $1$ on $E \times \mathrm{pt}$ and $\tfrac23$ on the fibre, so at $t = 1$ the surface $E \times \PP^1$ contracts to a curve, leaving $\AA^2 \times \PP^1$, and the second step contracts its fibres to $\AA^2$: a $K$-negative fibration. That middle target is $\Proj$ over $\Spec R_0$ of the algebra the sections generate, and its Stein factorization is certified trivial rather than computed: birational onto a normal image, so Zariski's main theorem applies. Polyhedra appears in the session only to write the four rings down; nothing after that uses the fan.

8. A program three steps long

In $N = \ZZ^2$ subdivide the fan of $\AA^2 = \mathrm{cone}(u_1,u_2)$, with $u_1 = (1,0)$ and $u_2 = (0,1)$, by

$$u_3 = (1,1), \qquad u_4 = u_1 + u_3 = (2,1), \qquad u_5 = u_1 + u_4 = (3,1),$$

each a star subdivision at a torus-fixed point of the exceptional curve before it, so $S_3$ is $\AA^2$ blown up three times, each time at a point of the last exceptional curve. Write $E_1, E_2, E_3$ for the divisors of $u_3, u_4, u_5$. The neighbours in the fan give $E_1^2 = E_2^2 = -2$ and $E_3^2 = -1$, and adjunction turns that into

$$K \cdot E_1 = K \cdot E_2 = 0, \qquad K \cdot E_3 = -1.$$

The input is $X = S_3 \times \AA^1$, projective over the affine base $\AA^3 = \AA^2 \times \AA^1$, polarized by the class $A$ with $A \cdot E_1 = A \cdot E_2 = A \cdot E_3 = 1$. Only $E_3$ is $K$-negative, and the same is true again after each contraction, so the program is forced, and every curve has $A \cdot C = 1$, so every threshold is $1$:

  1. the surface $E_3 \times \AA^1$ contracts to a line; the model becomes $S_2 \times \AA^1$;
  2. the surface $E_2 \times \AA^1$ contracts to a line; the model becomes $S_1 \times \AA^1 = \mathrm{Bl}_0(\AA^2) \times \AA^1$;
  3. the surface $E_1 \times \AA^1$ contracts to the axis $\{0\} \times \AA^1$; the model becomes $\AA^3$, where $K$ is nef.

Three divisorial steps. Both middle models are checked against ground truth built from their own polarizations, by a degree-preserving relabelling, and the last target is $\AA^3$, handed back as $\Proj$ of $\AA^3[t]$ so that the final nefness test has a $\Proj$ to read.

The fan is only how the input is written down: the session builds a subring of $k[a,b,c,t]$ and computes everything after that from the ring. The factor $\AA^1$ rather than $\PP^1$ keeps the structure morphism birational, which is what makes each threshold one intersection number on a fibre curve.

9. Three steps ending in a flip

The base is Example 7's: $\Spec R_0$ is not $\QQ$-Gorenstein, and the flipping side $Y$ of the circuit has one $\tfrac12(1,1,1)$ point, the cone $\langle v_1,v_2,v_4\rangle$ of multiplicity two. Two rays go in above $Y$:

$$w = (1,1,-1) \quad\text{interior to that cone},\qquad w_2 = v_2 + v_4 = (1,2,-2) \quad\text{on a facet of } \sigma.$$

The star subdivision at $w$ resolves the $\tfrac12(1,1,1)$ point, and $w_2$ adds one divisor over the facet. The input $X$ is the result: smooth, projective over $\Spec R_0$, of relative Picard rank three, polarized by $H = 4D_{v_4} + D_w + 3D_{w_2}$. Its wall curves have $(K \cdot C, H \cdot C) = (-1,1)$ on $\langle w,w_2\rangle$, $(-1,2)$ on $\langle v_1,w\rangle$ and $(0,1)$ on the rest, so the threshold is $1$ and one wall alone attains it:

  1. $t = 1$: the surface $D_{w_2}$ contracts to a curve; the model becomes the resolution of $Y$;
  2. $t = 1$: the surface $D_w$ contracts to the $\tfrac12(1,1,1)$ point; the model becomes $Y$;
  3. $t = \tfrac12$: the wall curve of the circuit contracts to the singular point of $\Spec R_0$. A curve, not a divisor, so the contraction is small and the step is a flip; the model becomes $Z$, where $K$ is nef.

What lets a flip come third is the circuit wall $\langle v_1,v_2\rangle$: it is $K$-trivial on $X$ and on the resolution and turns $K$-negative only on $Y$, so it cannot be contracted until the two divisors are gone. The fibre curves record that: $(K \cdot C, H \cdot C)$ runs $\{(-1,1),(0,1),(0,1)\}$, then $\{(-1,1),(0,1)\}$, then $\{(-1,1)\}$, and $\{(+1,1)\}$ after the flip — the sign change that makes it a flip and not a flop. It is also the only step whose relative canonical model is not the identity.

All four models are subrings of $k[a,b,c,t]$ over $R_0 = k[b,\, b^2c,\, a,\, abc,\, a^2c]$, written down as lists of monomials: the fan is in this account, not in the session. All three the program produces are checked against ground truth by a degree-preserving relabelling, and the last two are Example 7's $Y$ and $Z$, so this program reaches that flip from a ring three steps upstream.

Every push re-runs them and rebuilds this page, so a session that stops working stops the page from being published.

How to repeat these runs

You need Macaulay2 1.26.06 and a checkout with its submodules.

git clone --recurse-submodules https://github.com/takehikoyasuda/Coding_MMP
cd Coding_MMP
examples/run.sh                                   # all nine
examples/run.sh examples/01-projective-space.m2   # or one of them

Each run rewrites the .out transcript and the .time file next to its script. This page is built from those files, so it cannot quote output that Macaulay2 no longer prints.

What is not here

These nine examples finish. Some other inputs do not. Those cases were measured rather than guessed, and the measurements are in research-log/. What the implementation is tested and trusted to do is listed in docs/IMPLEMENTATION-STATUS.md.

The package's multigraded entry points are also not shown here. They take a multigraded variety as input, which the paper does not, so their examples belong with the package rather than with the paper.
