Worked examples

Seven worked examples that these packages compute, each with the Macaulay2 session that computes it.

Every example has two parts. First a short explanation of the mathematics. Then the session that does the computation. The sessions are real: the input files in examples/*.m2 are given to Macaulay2, and the output is saved exactly as Macaulay2 printed it. Each session is folded, with its main results shown on the fold. Click one to read all of it. The algorithms and their proofs are in the paper (arXiv:2603.13703).

In Examples 1 to 6, $X$ is a normal projective threefold and $R$ is its homogeneous coordinate ring. Example 7 relaxes that: some of the variables are given degree zero, their coordinates land in $R_0$, and $X = \Proj R$ is then projective over the affine $\Spec R_0$ rather than over a point. In all of them $K_X$ is the canonical divisor and $H$ is an ample Cartier divisor, ample over the base where there is one. The nef threshold is

$$\lambda = \min\{\,t \ge 0 \;:\; K_X + tH \text{ is nef}\,\}.$$

$X$ has to be normal and log terminal. The package assumes this. It does not check it.

In the paper, $X = \Proj R$ is monograded with $R_0 = k$. A bigraded ring appears only as the graph of a morphism, which is how the Stein factorization is computed. Examples 1 to 6 stay inside that setting. Example 7 keeps the single grading but drops $R_0 = k$, which is the relative setting; the package also has entry points that take a multigraded variety directly, and those go beyond the paper too, but this page does not use them.

1. Projective space

Here $X = \PP^3$ and $K_X = -4H$, where $H$ is the hyperplane class. The example asks three questions.

Is $K_X$ nef? No. The package tests the divisor $K_X + \tfrac12 H$. It checks the first six multiples of that divisor, and none of them is base point free. Six is enough: the effective base point free theorem says so for this case. So $K_X + \tfrac12 H$ is not nef. If $K_X$ were nef, then $K_X + \tfrac12 H$ would be nef too, because $H$ is ample. So $K_X$ is not nef.

What is the nef threshold? $K_X + tH = (t-4)H$, which is nef exactly when $t \ge 4$. So $\lambda = 4$, and the package finds $4$.

What does $K_X + \lambda H$ contract? $K_X + 4H = 0$. It is base point free, and its linear system sends $X$ to one point. This is a fibre type contraction, and the dimension drops by $3$. The MMP driver stops here, after one step, at a $K$-negative fibration.

2. A quintic threefold

$X$ is a smooth quintic in $\PP^4$. By adjunction $K_X = (5-5)H = 0$.

The package tests the pluricanonical divisors first. The first one is $K_X$ itself, and it is base point free. So $K_X$ is nef and $X$ is already a minimal model. The driver stops after zero steps.

3. The Segre threefold

$X = \PP^1 \times \PP^2$, embedded in $\PP^5$ by the Segre map. So $R$ is the quotient by the $2 \times 2$ minors of a $2 \times 3$ matrix of variables.

Here $K_X = \OO(-2,-3)$ and $H = \OO(1,1)$, so $K_X + tH = \OO(t-2,\,t-3)$. This is nef exactly when $t \ge 3$. The package computes $\lambda = 3$, and then computes the contraction given by

$$K_X + 3H = \OO(1,0).$$

The linear system of $\OO(1,0)$ is the projection $X \to \PP^1$. The package finds a fibre type contraction with target dimension $1$.

This time the target is not a point, so there is a Stein factorization to compute. The bound that certifies it is computed too, not assumed. Here the factorization is trivial, because $\mathrm{pr}_1$ already has connected fibres. Example 4 is the same $X$ with a map whose fibres are not connected.

4. A map with disconnected fibres

Stein factorization writes a map $f : Y \to W$ as $f = g \circ h$, where $h$ has connected fibres and $g$ is finite. In examples 3 and 5 the finite map $g$ turns out to be an isomorphism, so the factorization does nothing. This example is one where it does something.

Take $Y = \PP^1 \times \PP^2$ again, and the map

$$f : Y \to \PP^1, \qquad ([s_0:s_1],\,x) \longmapsto [s_0^2 : s_1^2].$$

Over a point of $\PP^1$ other than $[1:0]$ and $[0:1]$, the square map has two preimages, so the fibre of $f$ is two disjoint copies of $\PP^2$. Over those two points the fibre is one copy. The session checks this first, with no help from the package: it decomposes the fibre over $t_0 = t_1$ and gets two pieces, and the fibre over $t_1 = 0$ and gets one.

So $f$ does not have connected fibres, and the factorization has to be

$$Y \;\xrightarrow{\ h = \mathrm{pr}_1\ }\; \PP^1 \;\xrightarrow{\ g = \text{squaring}\ }\; \PP^1 .$$

The package finds exactly this. The middle variety $Z$ has dimension $1$, and $Y$ has dimension $3$, so $h$ has relative dimension $2$. The target also has dimension $1$, so $g$ is finite. And the Stein algebra has rank $2$ over the coordinate ring of the target, which is the degree of $g$. Degree $2$ means $g$ is not an isomorphism.

The same rank is $1$ in example 5, where $g$ is an isomorphism.

5. Blowing up a line, and a two-step program

$Y$ is the blow up of $\PP^3$ along a line, and $f : Y \to \PP^3$ is the map. The package is given one thing only: a parametrization of the graph of $f$. Nothing about $f$ is stated. The graph lives in a bigraded ring, where the first block of variables is the source and the second block is the target. This is how the paper writes a morphism.

Stein factorization. Write $f = g \circ h$, where $h$ has connected fibres and $g$ is finite. The package computes the coordinate ring of the middle variety $Z$. Two things come out of it. First, $\dim Z = 3$. Since $\dim Y = 3$ as well, the dimension does not drop, so $f$ is birational. Second, the ring of $Z$ is monograded, so the program can go on from $Z$ without any further work.

Is the contraction small? A birational contraction is called small when its exceptional locus has codimension $2$ or more. A small contraction needs a flip. A divisorial one does not. The package finds the exceptional locus as the support of $\bigwedge^2 \Omega_{Y/Z}$.

For this $f$ the exceptional locus is the exceptional divisor $E$. The package finds dimension $2$ and codimension $1$, so the contraction is divisorial.

The same test then runs on a small contraction, for comparison. Take the ordinary double point $\{g_0g_1 = g_2g_3\}$ and blow up the ideal $(g_0,g_2)$. This is the standard small resolution. Its exceptional locus is a curve, of dimension $1$ and codimension $2$, so that contraction is small. Example 6 computes a flip for a case like this one.

The program. The relative canonical model over $Z$ is the identity, so no flip is needed and the step is divisorial. The driver then continues from $Z$. It repeats example 1: $\lambda = 4$, and the contraction to a point. The whole program is

$$\mathrm{Bl}_L\PP^3 \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{divisorial}\ }\; \PP^3 \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{fibration}\ }\; \text{point},$$

two steps, ending at a $K$-negative fibration. Every ring in the chain is monograded, and the only bigraded ring is the graph of $f$.

6. A toric flip

Take four rays in $N = \ZZ^3$:

$$v_1 = e_1,\quad v_2 = e_2,\quad v_3 = e_3,\quad v_4 = (1,1,-2).$$

They satisfy one relation, $v_1 + v_2 = 2v_3 + v_4$. Four rays with a single relation of this shape form a circuit. The cone $\sigma$ they span can be triangulated in two ways, and the two results differ by a flip. The cone itself, with no triangulation, is the target of the flipping contraction.

$\sigma$ is not $\QQ$-Gorenstein. To see this, look for an $m$ with $\langle m, v_i \rangle = 1$ for all four rays. The first three rays force $m = (1,1,1)$, but $\langle (1,1,1), v_4 \rangle = 0$. So there is no such $m$.

The example builds this variety in Macaulay2. The dual cone has a Hilbert basis of five elements with three relations, and one more variable $w$ makes a projective threefold $W$. The flip is then computed as the relative canonical model

$$W^{+} = \Proj \bigoplus_{m \ge 0} H^0\bigl(W, \OO_W(mK_W)\bigr).$$

The session checks that this is a real flip: the result is not the identity and not an isomorphism, but it has the same dimension as $W$. The package also writes down the inverse rational map $W \dashrightarrow W^{+}$, checks that its equations hold, and finds its base locus $V(y_4,y_5)$. Recorded as an MMP step, this is a flipping step.

7. A flip the program finds by itself, over an affine base

Example 6 computes a flip, but it is handed the contraction: the flipping contraction is supplied and the relative canonical model is asked for. This one is given nothing but a ring. It decides that $K$ is not nef, finds the threshold, builds the contraction, checks that the contraction is small, and only then flips — a minimal model program with a flip in it, run from end to end.

What makes that possible is the base. The paper writes $X = \Proj R$ with $R_0 = k$, so $X$ is projective over a point, and the standard flips are not projective — a flipping contraction contracts a curve to a point of an affine variety. Giving some of the ambient variables degree zero puts their coordinates in $R_0$ instead, and $X = \Proj R$ becomes projective over the affine $\Spec R_0$. The three-fold flips then exist without being compactified first.

The input is the same circuit as in Example 6, read through its Cox ring. The relation $v_1 + v_2 = 2v_3 + v_4$ makes $\mathrm{Cl}(X) = \ZZ$ with $\deg(z_1,z_2,z_3,z_4) = (1,1,-2,-1)$, and the two triangulations are the two geometric invariant theory quotients: $Y$, where the monomials of nonpositive weight are the relevant ones, and $Z$, where the nonnegative ones are. On the wall curve $K_Y \cdot C = -1$ and $K_Z \cdot C = +1$, so $Y \to \Spec R_0$ is the flipping contraction and $Z$ is its flip.

$Y$ is presented by the second Veronese of its section ring. That ring needs a generator of degree two, and under a weighted grading $\Spec R \setminus V(B)$ is not a torsor: at the $\tfrac12(1,1,1)$ point the stabiliser is $\mu_2$, and a divisor can be locally free on the punctured cone without being invertible on $Y$. The Cartier test then over-reports — the same thing happens to $\OO(1)$ on $\PP(1,1,2)$ — and the canonical index comes back as $1$. The Veronese puts every positive-degree generator in degree one, and the index comes out as $2$, which is what the $\tfrac12(1,1,1)$ point forces.

The session then checks that the ring the program returns really is $Z$: a relabelling of the variables that preserves degrees carries one defining ideal onto the other. Three more inputs follow. $\mathrm{Bl}_0(\AA^3)$, as $\Proj$ of the Rees algebra of $(a,b,c)$, gives the other kind of birational step over an affine base — the contraction is not small, its exceptional locus being a surface, so the step is divisorial and the target is $\AA^3$. The weights $(1,1,-1,-3)$ give a flip of canonical index three, whose threshold is $\tfrac23$.

The last input is a program with two steps. Over a fixed affine base the contraction to $\Spec R_0$ is the end of the road: Algorithm 4 returns the whole relative canonical model at once, so one birational step exhausts the program. A longer one has to contract to a target that is neither the base nor a point. Take $X = \mathrm{Bl}_0(\AA^2) \times \PP^1$ over the affine base $\AA^2$, polarized by $\OO(1,3)$. Its two extremal rays over $\AA^2$ are the exceptional curve $E \times \mathrm{pt}$, with $K \cdot C = -1$ and $H \cdot C = 1$, and the fibre $\mathrm{pt} \times \PP^1$, with $K \cdot C = -2$ and $H \cdot C = 3$. The thresholds are $1$ and $\tfrac23$, so the larger is attained by $E$ alone and the contraction at $t = 1$ contracts $E \times \PP^1$ and nothing else, onto $\AA^2 \times \PP^1$. The second step then contracts that to $\AA^2$: a $K$-negative fibration, and the program stops.

The intermediate target is built as $\Proj$ over $\Spec R_0$ of the $R_0$-algebra the sections generate, which is again a presentation of the shape the program reads, so the next step starts from it with nothing to convert. The Stein factorization is not run on it; it is certified unnecessary. The morphism is certified birational onto its image — with $R$ generated in degree one over $R_0$, $K(X)$ is generated over $\mathrm{Frac}(R_0)$ by the ratios $u/u_1$ of the degree-one variables, and a degree-zero syzygy exhibiting $u_1 A = u B$ puts $u/u_1 = A/B$ in $\mathrm{Frac}(T)$ — and the image is checked normal, so Zariski's main theorem gives $\Phi_* \OO_X = \OO$ of the image. Here the image is $\AA^2 \times \PP^1$ on the nose: two coordinates of degree one, the base's two of degree zero, and no relations at all.

8. A program three steps long

Example 7 ends with a program of two steps, and two is the shortest length at which a sequence exists at all. In that program each step is also an end: the first contracts to an intermediate target, the second is the fibration that stops it. This one has a middle — a step that starts from a ring an earlier step built and hands another on.

The input is a surface times a line. In $N = \ZZ^2$ subdivide the fan of $\AA^2 = \mathrm{cone}(u_1,u_2)$, with $u_1 = (1,0)$ and $u_2 = (0,1)$, by

$$u_3 = (1,1), \qquad u_4 = u_1 + u_3 = (2,1), \qquad u_5 = u_1 + u_4 = (3,1),$$

each a star subdivision at a torus-fixed point of the exceptional curve before it, so $S_3 \to \AA^2$ is a chain of three smooth point blow-ups. Write $E_1, E_2, E_3$ for the divisors of $u_3, u_4, u_5$. The neighbours in the fan give $u_4 + u_2 = 2u_3$, $u_5 + u_3 = 2u_4$ and $u_1 + u_4 = u_5$, so $E_1^2 = E_2^2 = -2$ and $E_3^2 = -1$, and adjunction turns that into

$$K \cdot E_1 = K \cdot E_2 = 0, \qquad K \cdot E_3 = -1.$$

Exactly one of the three curves is $K$-negative — and the same is true again once $E_3$ is contracted, and again once $E_2$ is. So the program over $\AA^2$ is forced: contract $E_3$, then $E_2$, then $E_1$, and only then is $K$ nef. Three steps, with no choice made anywhere and no coincidence of thresholds to arrange. $X = S_3 \times \AA^1$ is that surface as a threefold, projective over the affine base $\AA^3 = \AA^2 \times \AA^1$, polarized by the relatively ample $A$ on $S_3$ with $A \cdot E_1 = A \cdot E_2 = A \cdot E_3 = 1$, whose sections over $k[a,b]$ are the four monomials $a^3, a^2b, ab^3, b^6$. Since every curve has $A \cdot C = 1$, the threshold is $1$ at each of the three steps.

Multiplying by $\AA^1$ rather than by $\PP^1$ is what keeps this affordable. The structure morphism stays birational, so $\dim R - \dim R_0 = 1$ and the negative-curve certificate of the relative setting applies: every threshold candidate is decided by an intersection number on a fibre curve rather than by a base-point-free test. With $\PP^1$ the same three-step program exists — divisorial, divisorial, then the fibration to $\AA^2$ — but the fibre has to be polarized heavily enough for its own threshold to stay below the two surface ones, and the smallest polarization that does needs $\OO(5)$ on the fibre: eighteen degree-one generators instead of four. That input was not carried through — its canonical index alone had not returned after eight minutes and ten gigabytes.

The session checks the two intermediate models against ground truth built independently from their own polarizations — on $S_2$ the class with $A \cdot E_1 = A \cdot E_2 = 1$, with sections $a^2, ab, b^3$, and on $S_1$ the class with $A \cdot E_1 = 1$, with sections $a, b$, which is the Rees algebra of $(a,b)$, so $\mathrm{Bl}_0(\AA^2) \times \AA^1$. A relabelling of the variables preserving degrees carries each defining ideal onto the other, so the models are the expected ones and not merely rings with their numbers. The last target is the affine base itself, handed back as $\Proj$ of $\AA^3[t]$ so that the final nefness test has a $\Proj$ to read, and $K$ is nef there on a base-point-free pluricanonical certificate.

9. Three steps ending in a flip

Example 8 gets its three steps from three divisorial contractions, and there Algorithm 4 is never asked for anything: a $\QQ$-Gorenstein target is its own relative canonical model, so every step's model is the identity. This program ends with a flip — a small contraction whose relative canonical model is a new variety, constructed rather than recognised. It is the first program here longer than one step with a flip in it.

The flip has to come last, and that is a property of the setting rather than of this input. Over an affine base the flipping contraction the program can carry out is the structure morphism $X \to \Spec R_0$, and that is the contraction at the threshold only when the relative Picard rank is one. A flip onto an intermediate target would ask for the relative canonical model of a base that is itself a $\Proj$ over $\Spec R_0$, whose Rees construction has degree-zero fibre variables and for which no heft vector exists. So a program with a flip in it has to spend its Picard rank on divisorial steps first.

The base is Example 7's. In $N = \ZZ^3$ the circuit $v_1 + v_2 = 2v_3 + v_4$ with $v_1 = (1,0,0)$, $v_2 = (0,1,0)$, $v_3 = (0,0,1)$, $v_4 = (1,1,-2)$ spans $\sigma$, and $\Spec R_0 = \Spec k[\sigma^\vee \cap M]$ is not $\QQ$-Gorenstein. Its flipping side $Y$ carries a $\tfrac12(1,1,1)$ point, the cone $\langle v_1,v_2,v_4\rangle$ of multiplicity two. Two rays go in above it:

$$w = (1,1,-1) \quad\text{interior to that cone},\qquad w_2 = v_2 + v_4 = (1,2,-2) \quad\text{on a facet of } \sigma.$$

The star subdivision at $w$ resolves the $\tfrac12(1,1,1)$ point, so the threefold $X$ that results is smooth, projective over $\Spec R_0$ of relative Picard rank three. Polarized by $H = 4D_{v_4} + D_w + 3D_{w_2}$ its wall curves have $(K \cdot C, H \cdot C) = (-1,1)$ on $\langle w,w_2\rangle$, $(-1,2)$ on $\langle v_1,w\rangle$ and $(0,1)$ on the rest, so the threshold is $1$, one wall alone attains it, and $D_{w_2}$ is what contracts. Nothing is tuned; the program is forced at every step.

What the configuration is for is what the circuit wall $\langle v_1,v_2\rangle$ does. On $X$, and on the model below it, that wall is $K$-trivial — the resolution's exceptional divisor absorbs the discrepancy — and it turns $K$-negative only on $Y$, once both divisorial steps have happened. The flip is invisible from the start and appears when the two divisors are gone. The fibre curves record it: $(K \cdot C, H \cdot C)$ runs $\{(-1,1),(0,1),(0,1)\}$ on $X$, then $\{(-1,1),(0,1)\}$, then $\{(-1,1)\}$ on $Y$, and $\{(+1,1)\}$ on the flip — the sign change that makes it a flip rather than a flop.

All four models are subrings of $k[a,b,c,t]$ over the same degree-zero part $R_0 = k[b,\, b^2c,\, a,\, abc,\, a^2c]$, each generated over it by the sections of the divisor the previous step hands on. The session checks all three models the program produces against ground truth built independently from their own polarizations, by a relabelling of the variables that preserves degrees. The last two are Example 7's $Y$ and $Z$: this program arrives at that flip from a ring three steps upstream of it, and $Z$ comes out as the Rees algebra of $(a,b)$ over $R_0$.

How to repeat these runs

You need Macaulay2 1.26.06 and a checkout with its submodules.

git clone --recurse-submodules https://github.com/takehikoyasuda/Coding_MMP
cd Coding_MMP
examples/run.sh                                   # all nine
examples/run.sh examples/01-projective-space.m2   # or one of them

Each run rewrites the .out transcript and the .time file next to its script. This page is built from those files, so it cannot quote output that Macaulay2 no longer prints.

What is not here

These nine examples finish. Some other inputs do not. Those cases were measured rather than guessed, and the measurements are in research-log/. What the implementation is tested and trusted to do is listed in docs/IMPLEMENTATION-STATUS.md.

The package's multigraded entry points are also not shown here. They take a multigraded variety as input, which the paper does not, so their examples belong with the package rather than with the paper.
